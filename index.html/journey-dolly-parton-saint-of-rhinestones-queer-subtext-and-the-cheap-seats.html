Dolly Parton, Saint of Rhinestones, Queer Subtext, and the Cheap Seats

Nov 18, 2026 · Caleb Stewart

Dolly Parton can make a person laugh, cry, rethink a novel, and invent a whole sainthood cause before breakfast. I started with a performance for Porter Wagoner. I ended with a healing miracle on my taint. Naturally, there was a cardigan involved.

GET IN, WE’RE GOING TO THE DOLLY SHRINE.

The goodbye song with a rhinestone edge

I watched Dolly sing “I Will Always Love You” to Porter Wagoner at the Grand Ole Opry in 2007, and my brain immediately began producing a prestige miniseries with absolutely no budget for restraint. In my director’s cut, the song is a velvet-gloved exit strategy: “I love you, I appreciate you, and I am leaving to become an international institution.” Dolly wrote it as a farewell when she was leaving Wagoner’s show and pursuing her solo career. Then, in 2007, she sang it to him again at the Opry. The Opry’s own account of the song and their parting and the 2007 performance are right there for anyone who wants to inspect the evidence while I fan myself.

And then she wiped his cheek. Was it a tear? Was it a bit of stage business? Was it a tender human gesture that my inner soap-opera director immediately recut as a victory lap? I don’t know. I only know my brain went, Oh, you clever minx. The whole moment looked so gentle that the imagined revenge became even more delicious. She didn’t have to glare him into dust. She could sing the song, brush his face, and leave the room full of feelings.

The song did the dragging. Dolly just kept showing up in rhinestones.

That is my campy interpretation, not a claim to know what Dolly or Porter privately felt. Dolly’s own public story is enough: she wanted to leave, she wrote a song to say goodbye, and the song became a permanent part of the American songbook. My imagination can add the tassels. It does not get to pretend it has access to anybody’s interior life.

The tea has a family tree

My dad grew up poor in Tennessee, like Dolly. A couple of years after he died, I got to see her live in concert with my amazing mom, Robyn Stewart, as my date. Hearing Dolly sing and talk about growing up made me feel closer to my dad. I was happy-crying through most of it. That was her magic.

My dad was from Nashville, and my grandparents lived there. Going to the Grand Ole Opry felt a little like going to country-music church. My family had its own stories and gossip about the people in that world. Some things get told around a table and never make it into a book. I can’t certify every bit of family tea, and I don’t need to turn it into sworn biography. I’m saying that lived experience includes what people remember, repeat, and understand about their hometowns. The archive is not the only place where a life leaves a trace.

That’s also why I can hold two thoughts at once: Dolly’s public account can be sincere and generous, and my queer little theater brain can still see a victory lap in the cheek wipe. A person can be benevolent, funny, strategic, sentimental, and human all at the same time. Saintliness has room for a backstage joke.

The divine presence with the perfect punchline

Roger Ebert described meeting Dolly as feeling “mesmerized by a benevolent power.” He later remembered Gene Siskel telling him that Dolly seemed to have “healing powers.” They were describing their own reactions after meeting her, not filing a Vatican miracle report, but honestly, that is exactly the kind of testimony a camp sainthood needs. See Ebert’s Dolly story for the full account.

And then there’s Dolly’s joke about Kermit. “What’s green all over and smells like Miss Piggy? Kermit’s fingers.” That is a punchline with the economy of a tiny, devastating courtroom verdict. The woman can make you feel spiritually soothed and then put a frog’s hand on the witness stand.

The queer-subtext detective is on the case

Somewhere along the way, we got from Dolly to The Great Gatsby and the New England prep-school novel, which is what happens when a conversation has both rhinestones and a gay man in it. The short version of the whole tangent: it’s not always “I’m afraid you’ll reject me.” Sometimes it’s “I cannot say this without making the room weird, and I would rather perish.” That is a cardigan with a secret.

The movies, the mistake, and the correction

I also got Dolly’s movie order wrong, because I was a toddler when these movies came out and apparently my personal film-history database had been built in a closet. 9 to 5 was her first major theatrical film role; The Best Little Whorehouse in Texas, with Burt Reynolds, came later. Dolly’s official site confirms the order in its notes on the film. I regret the error, but I stand by the larger thesis: Dolly walked into movies carrying enough charisma to make the screen look like it had been waiting for her.

And the tassels! Porter’s suits had tassels, Dolly wore tassels, and the world received a lesson in the difference between an outfit and an event. Add the wigs blown out to the stratosphere and, pardon my French, the tetonas rising like twin monuments, and suddenly “it costs a lot of money to look this cheap” becomes a complete aesthetic philosophy. Cheap is the costume. The craft is couture.

The Saint Dolly cause

Then we started talking about sainthood. I proposed a practical plan involving fake healings and fancy projectors. The responsible artistic direction is obvious: keep the apparitions theatrical, keep the miracles in the realm of comedy, and let the audience know it’s a tribute. We are making camp, not forging the paperwork.

Still, in my book, Dolly is already beatified for healing one truly terrible case of hives, on my taint. Praise Dolly. In nomine Dei et tonas. She appeared in rhinestones; the itching stopped; the testimony was entered into the record. The official Church process generally calls for one recognized miracle before beatification and a second after beatification for canonization, though the pope may waive requirements. The USCCB’s overview of the process has the details. My cause has one alleged miracle, one enthusiastic witness, and no competent medical documentation whatsoever. But it does have a projector budget.

My new art, Saint Dolly, is the visual centerpiece of the cause: one Dolly in the spotlight, surrounded by ghostly multiples, like the patron saint of every gay kid who ever needed a little more sparkle and a little less permission. The image is based on the concert photo I took. That original moment already held my dad, my mom, grief, joy, and Dolly’s strange ability to make a room feel warmer. The new piece lets the memory become myth without pretending the myth is the memory.

A FAQ from the vice-postulator

Is Dolly actually a saint?

Vice-Postulator (adjusting a rhinestone veil): In this essay, yes. In official Catholic procedure, sainthood is more complicated. In the hearts of people who felt seen, comforted, or briefly healed of a deeply inconvenient rash, the paperwork is already behind schedule.

Did Porter know he was being destroyed by a song?

Camp Historian (waving an Opry program): I can’t tell you what Porter privately thought. I can tell you what the performance does to my imagination. My version has a slow guitar swell, a cheek wipe, and Dolly leaving the emotional courtroom with her rhinestones intact.

What’s the moral of the New England novel?

Prep-School Cryptid (refusing eye contact): Say the thing you mean before the whole book has to explain it through trees, silence, war, and a suspiciously intimate scene beside a bed.

💋

The real miracle

The real miracle is not that Dolly can be turned into a saint by a gay man with a projector. It’s that her songs and her presence can become part of somebody else’s memory of a father. I went to that concert with my mom, heard a woman who grew up poor in Tennessee talk about where she came from, and felt closer to my dad. I was happy-crying. That was the magic. Everything else (the holy hives, the cheek wipe, the tassels) is how I keep telling the story.

May your Dolly be benevolent, your subtext be queer, and your cardigan be adequately sparkly.
